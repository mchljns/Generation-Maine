# Generation Maine: Brand platform

This page decides what the identity has to do before anyone draws another mark. Every design route from here on is judged against it.

## Why this page exists

We made seven rounds of marks from seven different ideas. That happens when the strategy is not locked. Our own strategy (`01-strategy.md`) already said the answer: the creators lead, and the logo signs the work instead of covering it. Most rounds since v4 drifted from that by chasing a big symbol. This page puts the strategy back in charge.

## The platform

**What it is.** Short videos and a Substack made by 8 to 12 young Maine creators about how economic rules shape their lives. An initiative of Maine Policy Institute, and open about it.

**Who it is for.**
- First: Mainers roughly 18 to 30, many of them new or recent voters. They live on TikTok, Instagram and YouTube Shorts.
- Second: older Mainers who follow state news, the kind of reader The Maine Wire has. They read Substack and Facebook.

**The tension.** "I want to build my life here. Can I?" It is a real question with no single answer. Some creators will be struggling. Some will be doing fine. Both belong.

**The brand's role.** Hand young Mainers the camera and get out of the frame. The brand is the host, not the narrator.

**Promise.** You will hear young Mainers describe their own lives in their own words, and you will always know who is behind the project.

**Personality.** Local, straightforward, curious, warm. Never preachy, partisan, glossy, nostalgic or gloomy.

**Voice.** Plain words, one idea per sentence, stories instead of positions. Short headlines. No hype, no em dashes, no italics, and no invented facts.

## Where the identity actually lives

Most people will meet Generation Maine inside a creator's video, not on the website. That fact should drive every design decision.

| Place | What the brand gets there | What that demands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A creator's video, first seconds | A corner, for a moment, under the platform's own buttons and captions | A small mark that signs without looking like an ad |
| Lower third | A few seconds, bottom of frame | Name and town first, brand second |
| End card | A few seconds, full frame | Clear handles and the Maine Policy Institute line |
| Profile grid | Nine cover images seen at once | Covers that read as one series at thumbnail size |
| Avatar | A small circle next to other accounts | A mark that holds up at about 110 px and 32 px |
| Collab posts on creators' own accounts | Their audience, their feed | Light enough that creators are proud to post it |
| Substack header and emails | The older reader's first impression | Credible and editorial, not a youth-brand costume |
| Website | A splash page with no photos at first | Looks finished with type and color alone |

## The test: eight criteria

Every route must pass all eight, shown on real mockups rather than on a presentation board.

1. **Signs, does not cover.** Sits in a corner of a creator's video without reading as an ad.
2. **Survives the crop.** Holds up as a 110 px circle avatar and a 32 px favicon.
3. **Carries a person.** On content, the creator's name, town and words are the most visible brand elements.
4. **Neutral.** Reads as no party, campaign, ballot or nostalgia.
5. **Both audiences.** Feels native on TikTok and credible on Substack.
6. **Stands without photos.** The site and Substack look finished before the photo shoot.
7. **Ownable.** Not a common category icon. Clear of Baxter, Green Falls, 76crew and The Maine Wire.
8. **Easy to make.** A creator or a staffer can make a correct cover or lower third in Canva in ten minutes.

## Identity architecture

1. **Master brand.** The Generation Maine wordmark, a small avatar mark drawn from it, color and type. Quiet and consistent.
2. **Creator frame.** Name, hometown and the creator's own words, placed the same way everywhere. This is where the brand is most visible.
3. **Series layer.** Named formats that sit on the frame, for example "The Math" or "Reasons to Stay." Each can have its own small look without changing the master brand.
4. **Endorsement.** "An initiative of Maine Policy Institute," always readable, never hidden.

## Two routes for the next round

Both keep the wordmark base from v1 and v5 and the Spruce and Marigold palette. They differ in what makes the creator frame ownable.

**Route A: In their words.** The creator's own sentence is the graphic element. A distinct caption and quote style carries every cover, lower third and page. The brand looks like people talking.

**Route B: Hometown.** The creator's town name is the graphic element, set large, the way a team jersey carries a city. A season becomes a list of real towns: Rumford, Presque Isle, Biddeford. Rural readers and young people both claim their towns.

**How we will show them.** The same eight mockups for each route, and no boards:
1. A creator video frame with the platform's buttons and captions laid over it
2. A lower third mid-video
3. The end card
4. A profile grid of nine covers
5. The avatar at 110 px among other accounts
6. A Substack header and email
7. The website hero with no photo
8. A collab post on a creator's own account

## The on-the-nose test

Describe any new element in one sentence. If the sentence restates the name or the brief, it is on the nose and it goes. The name already says young and Maine. The look adds confidence, not a picture of the name.

Every earlier round failed this test: the record dot said "video," the doorway and the farm said "building a life," the ledger said "economic rules," Route A said "their words" and Route B said "Maine."

## Round one result: Signature

Routes A and B were built on the mockups (`brand/kit/`). Both passed most criteria and both restated the brief. The recommended direction is Signature, which has no metaphor and runs on five rules:

1. One idea per frame.
2. Big, tight type anchored low left.
3. Full color fields in a fixed order: Spruce, Birch, Pine, with Marigold as a field once in a set of nine.
4. Marigold appears once per frame, usually as the dot that ends a headline.
5. The creator's name and town are plain information, never a device.

Its one partial score is ownability. That comes from using the same rules every time. Caption blocks from Route A stay available inside videos as a content tool, not as the brand.

## Round two: Signature, tightened

Signature was checked against real editorial and mission-led screens on Refero (`03-pressure-test.md`). Nothing new was added. The five rules now have numbers: a fixed margin, a fixed color order, one job for Marigold and one style for the credit line. The bug is the wordmark alone. Ownability is still partial, and closer.

## Research before design

We cannot talk to people ourselves, so these scripts are ready for someone on the team to run.

**Three creator calls, 20 minutes each.**
- What town do you say you are from when someone asks?
- What is one thing you love about living here, and one thing that is hard?
- Would you post a video with this frame on your own account? What would make you not want to? (Show routes A and B.)
- Which of these two looks more like you?
- What words would you never want next to your name?

**Gut check with eight people: five young Mainers and three older news readers.** Show the avatar and end card for three seconds each, then ask:
- Who do you think made this?
- Would you stop scrolling for it?

"Who made this" is the most important question. If people answer "a political group" or "an ad," the route fails criterion 1 or 4.

**Questions for Garrick.**
- How does Maine Policy Institute describe its own work in one sentence?
- Are there rules for how the institute's name or logo appears next to a project?
- Who will make the covers and lower thirds day to day, and in what tool?
- Are the creators already chosen? If so, which towns are they from?
