# Two Maines, from a young person's seat

Research memo for Generation Maine. October 2, 2026. Built from published reporting, state data and polls found by web search. Article pages could not be opened from this environment, so figures come from search summaries and are marked for confirmation where the primary source was not read in full. Nothing here is invented. Where a number needs a second look it carries `[CONFIRM]`.

## The short version

1. The divide is real and it is two different problems. In the south a young person can find the job and cannot afford the place. In the north a young person can afford the place and cannot find the job.
2. Both halves now share one complaint: the cost of living. Three quarters of Mainers call the state unaffordable and 30 percent say they are likely to leave within five years, with housing and cost of living the reason for two thirds of them (UNH Pine Tree State Poll, July 2026).
3. Young people do not talk about "two Maines." They say "down south," "up north," "the County," "Portland." The phrase belongs to editorial pages and economic reports going back to the 1990s.
4. The pull to stay is rising, not falling, among the young people surveyed. Sixty three percent of 2024 Mitchell Scholars planned to live in Maine after college, the highest since the question was first asked. Sixteen percent of Bowdoin's 2024 class planned to stay, up from 10 percent in 2018.
5. The rules Generation Maine films are not the same rules in both places. South: rent, zoning, what a lease allows. North: the license, the drive, the seasonal job, the childcare that does not exist.

Reading the table: the money moved south and the room stayed north. A young person in Biddeford and a young person in Presque Isle are both doing math, but the columns are different.

## The south, as a young person sees it

The south is thriving and that is the problem. Portland got cooler, employers came, remote workers arrived with salaries set elsewhere, and the entry price rose past what a first job pays. The sentiment is of being priced out of your own hometown by people who love it as much as you do.

- A full-time worker needs $28.42 an hour for a two-bedroom; the median wage is $24.19. For someone at 22 on a first salary the gap is wider.
- "Portlyn" is the word locals use for what happened, and it is not affectionate. Native-born share of the city fell from 72 percent to 40 percent in forty years.
- The resentment has a target: people from away, landlords, the town that will not permit the building. The Island Institute traces "from away" to decades of locals being treated as the help and watching places fill past what they were built for.
- The response is not usually leaving the state. It is moving back in with parents, commuting from Sanford or Lewiston, working a second job, or leaving for New Hampshire, which is drawing Maine's young workers for the same affordability reasons and now struggling to keep them (NHFPI, May 2026).
- Young people in the south are more optimistic about advancement than older Mainers. In the July 2026 Siena survey for the Maine Trust for Local News, 18 to 34 year olds were more bullish about job advancement than any older group, while 85 percent of all respondents viewed affordable housing availability negatively.

What this sounds like in their words: I have the job. I cannot have the apartment. The lease, the fee, the deposit, the zoning fight at town hall, the landlord who would rather rent to summer people.

## The north, as a young person sees it

The north is affordable and that is the problem. The house is $150,000 to $180,000, and the job that would pay for it closed with the mill, or needs a license that costs a year, or is forty minutes away.

- Aroostook lost more people than any county in the last census. Presque Isle and Caribou fell more than 9 percent each. The reason given most often by people who left was a lack of economic opportunity, and most did not sever ties (Bangor Daily News, 2020).
- The Katahdin region lost its biggest employer when Great Northern Paper closed in 2008, and young people there have been "scrambling to find a new path" ever since (NPR). Rumford and the other mill towns tell the same story at a smaller scale.
- Washington County high school juniors and seniors show a dominant interest in starting their own businesses, from food trucks to landscaping to locksmithing, and that interest is "often paired with a desire to stay," which the Sunrise County Economic Council calls a push against "the historical narrative that they must leave to be successful" (January 2026).
- Northern youth lean on family. Aroostook graduates have historically stayed close to home, and the region's own recruiting pitch (Opportunities Aroostook) is built on young adults returning with skills and the cheapest houses in the state.
- The enrollment surge at the University of Maine at Presque Isle, from about 1,160 students in 2022 to over 4,000, is mostly online adult learners, not local eighteen year olds. It changes the campus's finances more than the town's youth count. `[CONFIRM against the Maine Monitor piece, Aug 2026]`
- Child care is the quiet rule. Seventy percent of young children in remote rural Maine live in a licensed child care desert. A young parent in Fort Kent is not weighing a job offer against rent. They are weighing it against whether anyone can watch the baby.
- The villain is different. It is Augusta, the state rule, the mill owner who left, the summer economy that pays in July and not in February.

What this sounds like in their words: I could buy a house here. There is nothing to pay for it with. I drive forty minutes each way. The license took a year and the class cost more than my car.

## A third Maine: Lewiston

Androscoggin is the youngest county in the state by median age (40.5), largely because of immigrant and refugee families. Their kids are going to college in rising numbers, and the open question reported from Lewiston is whether they will come back and whether there will be jobs if they do. For Generation Maine this is a distinct voice: a young person whose family chose Maine, who feels the same housing squeeze, and who hears "from away" differently.

## What they share

- Cost of living is the unifying complaint. 56 percent of Mainers said they were worse off financially than a year earlier (June 2026, Maine Wire citing a Pan Atlantic survey `[CONFIRM: polling firm]`).
- Licensing touches everyone. About one in four Maine workers needs an occupational license. The average license for a low or moderate income job takes 323 days of training. Cosmetology needs 1,500 hours and a program costs about $15,279 on average (Institute for Justice, License to Work). This is a story that plays the same in Sanford and in Machias.
- They vote. Maine had the second-highest youth turnout in the nation in 2024, at 60 percent (CIRCLE, Tufts). This is not a disengaged audience.
- Mental health is improving from a low point. The share of high school students who felt sad or hopeless for two weeks or more fell from 35 percent in 2023 to 28 percent in 2025 (Maine Integrated Youth Health Survey).
- The will to stay is up. Mitchell Scholars (63 percent planning to live in Maine), Bowdoin (16 percent, up from 10), Colby (about 10 percent of graduates since 2014 stayed). The reasons cited are community and the professional connections made in college.
- The old line still hurts. "A great place to be from. Not to live in, but to be from." (Henry Abbott, Bowdoin Orient, 2023.) The project exists to make that line untrue.

## What this means for Generation Maine

**The roster is right, and it needs to say so.** The nine placeholder towns are Skowhegan, Presque Isle, Biddeford, Machias, Lewiston, Rumford, Belfast, Fort Kent and Sanford. That is two Aroostook towns, one Washington County, three mill or former mill towns, two York County towns in Portland's commuter ring, one coastal town, one immigrant city, and no Portland. Keep it that way, and say it in the about section: the stories come from the towns, not the city.

**The story list already splits the way the state does.** Finding a place, Moving out and Coming home are southern stories. The commute, Winter work, Two jobs and Doing the math are northern stories. Starting a shop and Who stays belong to both. When real creators are cast, protect that balance.

**The hero line lands twice.** "Young Mainers on building a life here" means affording to stay in the south and finding the work to stay in the north. Do not resolve that into one meaning. It is the project's range.

**Do not say "two Maines."** Young people do not use the phrase, and it carries thirty years of newspaper baggage. Let the towns and the numbers show the divide. The site's language should be "where they live," "the town," "the drive."

**Film the rule, not the villain.** The two halves blame different people. The brand promise is the rule and the number, in the creator's words. A lease clause, a license fee, a permit timeline, a child care waitlist. That keeps the project credible in both halves and keeps it out of the from-away fight, which no brand wins.

**The funder line needs the same care.** Maine Policy Institute is a free-market policy shop and the project's name is on every page. The research above cuts both ways: housing supply and licensing are rule stories that fit that frame; child care deserts and mill closures are not rule stories in the same sense. Publishing both kinds is what makes the project a chronicle rather than a campaign. `[CONFIRM: with MPI how far the story list can run]`

**Cast one who left and one who came back.** The Aroostook finding is that people leave and keep the ties. A creator who moved to Portland from Presque Isle, and one who moved back to Rumford from Boston, cover more of the real sentiment than nine who never left.

**The newsletter is where the numbers live.** The video says "it took eleven signatures." The Substack post lists them. Every claim in this memo that a creator repeats should carry its source in the post.

**For the two concepts.** Signature's weight suits the southern story: loud, urban, a record light on a feed. Bark & Sky's quiet suits the northern story: paper, horizon, the long view. The client is choosing a voice for both halves at once. Whichever wins, the creators' own footage will carry the other half.

## Gaps

- Article pages at the Press Herald, Bangor Daily News, Maine Monitor, Maine Public and Sun Journal could not be opened from this environment. Figures from them come from search summaries and should be checked against the pages before any number goes in a video.
- No age breakdown was found for the 30 percent likely to leave. The UNH Survey Center report would have it.
- Rural broadband was not covered.
- No direct quotes from young people in the north were captured. The Bangor Daily News Aroostook piece (March 2020) and the Press Herald piece on young people and costs (September 2026) have them and are the first two things to read with a subscription.
